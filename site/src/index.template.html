Probabilistic matrix factorization on MovieLens

Five factorization models and four non-factorization baselines, evaluated on MovieLens 100K and 1M over repeated random splits with confidence intervals. The study measures what each modelling choice is worth — latent dimensionality, regularization strength, link function, optimizer, and a full Bayesian treatment — and where the remaining error lives.

Best model — test RMSE, MovieLens 100K
Strongest baseline — additive user and item offsets, no latent factors
Cost scaling — log–log exponent in \(N\) at fixed model size

Model

Each user \(i\) carries a latent vector \(\mathbf{u}_i \in \mathbb{R}^d\) and each item \(j\) a vector \(\mathbf{v}_j \in \mathbb{R}^d\). An observed rating is modelled as a noisy inner product of the two, giving the likelihood in the masthead, and zero-mean spherical Gaussian priors are placed on both factor sets:

\[p(U \mid \sigma_U^2)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{N}\big(\mathbf{u}_i \mid \mathbf{0},\,\sigma_U^2\mathbf{I}\big), \qquad p(V \mid \sigma_V^2)=\prod_{j=1}^{m}\mathcal{N}\big(\mathbf{v}_j \mid \mathbf{0},\,\sigma_V^2\mathbf{I}\big).\]

Maximising the log posterior in \(U\) and \(V\) with the hyperparameters held fixed is equivalent to minimising a regularized squared error over the observed entries alone:

\[E=\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m} I_{ij}\big(R_{ij}-\mathbf{u}_i^{\top}\mathbf{v}_j\big)^2 +\tfrac{\lambda_U}{2}\lVert U\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^2 +\tfrac{\lambda_V}{2}\lVert V\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^2, \qquad \lambda_U=\frac{\sigma^2}{\sigma_U^2},\quad \lambda_V=\frac{\sigma^2}{\sigma_V^2}.\]

Two consequences follow. The regularization strength is a ratio of variances rather than a free parameter, which is what distinguishes this from ridge-penalized factorization. And because the sums run over observed pairs only, a gradient step costs \(O(|\mathcal{O}|\,d)\) rather than \(O(nm\,d)\) — the basis of the method's claim to scale with the data rather than with the matrix.

The Bayesian extension replaces the fixed prior variances with Gaussian–Wishart hyperpriors, \(\mathbf{u}_i \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_U, \Lambda_U^{-1})\) and \(\mathbf{v}_j \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_V, \Lambda_V^{-1})\), and integrates over both factors and hyperparameters by Gibbs sampling. Every conditional is conjugate, so a sweep is exact sampling and the regularization strength is inferred rather than tuned.

Experimental setup

Ratings are split \(70/10/20\) into training, validation and test partitions. Model selection and early stopping use validation only; the test partition is scored once per fitted model. The headline comparison is repeated over five random splits, the ablations over three, and every figure carries a Student-\(t\) 95% interval.

Baselines are included because a root-mean-squared error carries no meaning in isolation. The relevant question is how much a factorization buys over predicting a global mean, a per-user mean, a per-item mean, or a damped additive combination of user and item offsets — the last of which is a strong model with no interaction term at all.

Analytic gradients are verified against central finite differences of the objective for every variant: both link functions, both regularization conventions, and with and without bias terms.

Training cost

Fitting \(\log t = a + b \log N\) over subsamples of both datasets makes the method's efficiency claim measurable: \(b = 1\) is exactly linear. Read the two within-dataset fits. Subsampling varies \(N\) while holding the user and item counts fixed, which is the comparison the claim concerns; pooling also varies those counts, and since the momentum update touches every factor row on every step, the larger dataset pays a constant surcharge at equal \(N\) that lifts the pooled exponent without any curvature being present.

Findings

  • The Bayesian treatment is the strongest model tested, and its advantage over MAP estimation is concentrated in the sparse-profile strata rather than spread evenly across users.
  • Additive user and item offsets recover most of the achievable accuracy. The interaction term contributes a real but smaller increment, which is the quantity a factorization has to be judged on.
  • Regularization strength is the largest single hyperparameter effect. Latent dimensionality matters considerably less once early stopping is in place, which is itself acting as capacity control.
  • The logistic link bounds predictions to the rating range by construction, but the accuracy it adds over simply clipping an unbounded model is small. Its useful \(\lambda\) is an order of magnitude below the identity model's, because the gradient carries a factor \(g(x)\big(1-g(x)\big) \leq \tfrac{1}{4}\) on the data term and not on the prior.
  • Training cost is linear in the number of observations at fixed model size, as claimed. The dense momentum update adds a term proportional to \(n_{\text{users}} + n_{\text{items}}\) that the gradient itself does not carry.

This study began as a re-execution of an earlier report by the same author, which quoted a single test RMSE of 1.0109 with no baseline, interval or seed. That figure is reachable only by under-training and sits behind the additive baseline reported here; the present numbers supersede it. Details are in the paper's appendix.
